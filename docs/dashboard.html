What would actually happen if you jumped into a hole through the Earth?

Drawn Out (working name)

Script

Jump into a hole through the Earth, and you'd pop out in thirty-eight minutes.

The hole would be about twelve thousand seven hundred kilometers long.

The deepest hole anyone has dug? Twelve kilometers. About a tenth of a percent of the way.

So let's pretend. No air, no lava, no spinning Earth.

You jump. Gravity pulls you down, faster and faster.

By the center, you're doing more than eight kilometers a second.

Then gravity flips. Now it's behind you, slowing you down.

You rise up the other side and stop, right at the top.

Grab the edge. Miss it, and you fall all the way back. And back again.

Textbooks say the trip takes forty-two minutes. But that assumes Earth is the same density all the way through.

It isn't. The crust is about three grams per cubic centimeter. The center, about thirteen.

Redo the sum with the real Earth, measured by earthquakes, and you get thirty-eight minutes.

Weirder still: pretend gravity never changes on the way down. You get almost the same answer.

Thirty-eight minutes. Door to door.

What would actually happen if you fell into quicksand?

Drawn Out (working name)

Script

In films, quicksand swallows people whole. Physics says you'd stop at your waist.

Quicksand is fine sand, clay and salt water. Leave it alone, and it slowly thickens.

Step on it, and it turns to liquid. The harder you push, the runnier it gets.

So you sink. And thrashing makes you sink faster.

The Keys to the Internet (Sort Of)

Pilot long-form 5:40 (video file not in the repo)

Script

Four times a year, a handful of strangers travel to one of two very boring-looking buildings. One is in Culpeper, Virginia. The other is in El Segundo, California. They type in a PIN. They swipe a smartcard. They scan their hand, and then their eye. They open a safe. And inside that safe are the keys to the internet.

Sort of. And in two weeks, for only the second time in history, they're changing the locks.

You might have seen the headlines. Meet the seven people who hold the keys to the internet. It's a fantastic headline. It is also, according to the people who actually hold the keys, wrong. So let's figure out what these keys really do, who has them, and what happens if someone loses one.

Every time you visit a website, your computer has to ask where that website lives. A name like wikipedia dot org means nothing to a computer. It needs a number, an IP address. The system that translates one into the other is called the Domain Name System, or DNS. It's basically the internet's phone book.

And for a long time, that phone book had a problem. It trusted whoever answered. If a hacker could slip in a fake answer first, your computer would happily believe it, and send you to a fake version of your bank. In two thousand and eight, a researcher named Dan Kaminsky showed that this was much, much easier than anyone thought.

The fix is called DNSSEC. Every answer in the phone book now comes with a cryptographic signature, a kind of tamper-proof seal. And every level vouches for the level below it. Wikipedia dot org is vouched for by dot org. And dot org is vouched for by the very top of the whole system: the root.

But who vouches for the root? Nobody. There's nothing above it. The root's signature is the one thing the entire system has to take on faith. Which means the key that makes that signature had better be very, very well protected.

That key is called the Key Signing Key. It lives inside machines called hardware security modules: tamper-proof boxes designed to wipe themselves if anyone tries to break in. There are copies on both coasts, more than four thousand kilometres apart. The key never leaves these boxes. Ever.

But to wake one of these machines up, you need smartcards. The smartcards are sealed in bags, inside safe-deposit boxes, inside a safe. And every safe-deposit box needs two keys. One is held by ICANN, the organisation that runs all this. The other is held by a crypto officer.

There are fourteen crypto officers, seven for each coast. They're volunteers from the internet community, from all over the world, and at least three of them have to show up for anything to happen. Then there are seven more people, called recovery key share holders, who each hold a piece of the backup. Five of them are needed, but only if every single machine is destroyed.

So the famous seven people? It's actually twenty-one people. And not one of them can do anything alone.

The ceremony itself is gloriously over the top. The officers pass through layer after layer of security. Two staff members, who must always stay together, enter a separate safe room to fetch the equipment. Every tamper-evident bag is checked against a serial number written down months earlier. The laptop they use has no battery and no hard drive. It boots from a DVD. And every single step is read aloud from a script, filmed, and streamed live on the internet.

Then, three officers insert their smartcards, the machine wakes up, and it signs the keys the internet will use for the next three months. That's it. That's the whole ritual. It's designed so that even if one in twenty participants were dishonest, the odds of a group of them stealing the key are less than one in a million.

Of course, even the most secure room on the internet has a weakness. In February twenty twenty, during routine maintenance in California, the lock on one of the safes failed. Not hacked. Just broken. The only fix was to call a locksmith and drill through the safe, which took most of two days. It was the first time in ten years that a ceremony had to be rescheduled.

Two months later, a pandemic grounded flights around the world. So the crypto officers did the only sensible thing. They mailed their keys, sealed in tamper-evident bags, and watched the ceremony on a video call. And just to be safe, the machine signed nine months of keys at once, instead of three.

So, can these people turn off the internet? No. The keys only work inside those two buildings. They only protect one thing: the signature at the top of the phone book. And if every key vanished tomorrow, ICANN could buy a new machine and restore everything from encrypted backups. Annoying? Very. Apocalyptic? No.

Which brings us back to changing the locks. The first root key was created in twenty ten. It was supposed to be replaced in twenty seventeen, but that got delayed by a whole year, because too many systems weren't ready. The switch finally happened on October the eleventh, twenty eighteen. A new key was made in twenty twenty-four. And on October the eleventh, twenty twenty-six, it takes over.

If everything goes to plan, you won't notice a thing. Which is exactly the point. Nobody can switch off the internet. But four times a year, a few volunteers fly across the world, scan their eyeballs, and read a script out loud next to a safe, just so the rest of us never have to think about it.
